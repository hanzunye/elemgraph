# Q4 and Q1 on the Apple M2, 2026-11-11

This is the first run of `bench/connectivity.cpp` (Q4). BFS (Q1) was run again, now also with 2^19
and 2^21 vertices. Raw results are in [`2026-11-11.json`](2026-11-11.json).

## Setup

- Machine and settings as on [2026-11-04](2026-11-04.md): Apple M2, on AC power, no other
  applications open, QoS class `USER_INTERACTIVE`.
- clang 21.1.8 with libc++, `-O3 -DNDEBUG`, commit `e30cc0a`.
- `elemgraph_bench --benchmark_repetitions=10 --benchmark_report_aggregates_only=true`. All tables
  show medians of 10 repetitions. The full run took 25 minutes.
- Variation within the run: the coefficient of variation is at most 2.2 %, with two exceptions.
  - With 2^12 vertices it reaches 7.3 %.
  - BFS on the adjacency list reaches 4.6 % for R-MAT with 2^18 vertices and 2.7 % with 2^19.
- Variation between runs, compared with 11/04: BFS on CSR is within about 1 % for 2^18 vertices
  and more, and within 6 % for 2^14 and 2^16. With 2^12 vertices, the BFS times for G(n,m) and
  R-MAT differ by up to a factor of 2 between the two runs. Single results at that size should not
  be trusted, and none of the conclusions below rests on them.

output.
- *DFS* is the time of that search alone.
- *Lowpoint* and *Chains* are the times of each algorithm after the search, in µs.
- *Chains / lowpoint* compares these two times; values below 1 (bold) mean the chain
  decomposition is faster.
- *With DFS* compares the whole computations: (DFS + chains) / (DFS + lowpoint).

### Reading

1. **After the search, the lowpoint algorithm is faster in 20 of 24 cases.** It is faster for
   every grid and R-MAT graph, and for G(n,m) up to 2^18 vertices. The chain decomposition is
   faster only for G(n,m) with 2^19 vertices and more, and only by 3–5 %.
2. **The gap depends on how well the graph fits in the caches.**
   - On the grid, neighbours have nearby ids, so memory is read almost in order. There the chain
     decomposition takes 1.20–1.28× as long at every size.
   - On G(n,m) and R-MAT, the gap is 1.3–1.9× with 2^14 to 2^18 vertices. It shrinks once the
     graph outgrows the caches: G(n,m) changes sides at 2^19 vertices, and R-MAT ends at 1.07×
     with 2^22 vertices.

   A guess, not yet checked:
   - In cache, the number of instructions decides. The chain decomposition does more work per
     vertex than the lowpoint algorithm: it makes a second pass over all vertices to find the
     bridges, scans the list of the lower end of every bridge again, and climbs the chains. R-MAT
     has a bridge for about every seventh vertex, which fits its gap being the largest (about
     2×).
   - Out of cache, both algorithms wait for the same kind of random accesses to per-vertex arrays,
     and the extra instructions no longer matter.

   Hardware counters (instructions and cache misses, `perf stat` on the x86 laptop) can check
   this.

   *Checked on the x86 laptop the same day: there the chain decomposition executes 11–13 % fewer
   instructions than the lowpoint algorithm, not more. See
   [`../x86-laptop/2026-11-11.md`](../x86-laptop/2026-11-11.md).*
3. **With the search included, the difference is small.** The whole computation with the chain
   decomposition takes 0.98–1.33× as long as with the lowpoint algorithm. With 2^20 vertices and
   more, the range is 0.98–1.09×.
4. **The search costs more than either algorithm after it.** With 2^14 vertices and more, the DFS
   takes 1.5–1.6× as long as the lowpoint part on the grid, 1.1–1.7× on G(n,m) and 1.0–2.0× on
   R-MAT. So for Q4, a faster DFS
   would help both algorithms more than any change to either of them. Compared with BFS on the
   same graph (Q1 below, 2^14 vertices and more), the DFS is 2.3–3.5× slower on G(n,m) and
   2.7–3.3× slower on R-MAT. On the grid it is 1.0–2.3× slower.

### Reading

1. The peak of 11/04 reproduces: 1.87× and 1.84× with 2^20 vertices. The new sizes show that it
   covers exactly 2^19 and 2^20 vertices. With 2^18 and 2^21 vertices the ratio is 1.2–1.35×.
2. This fits the guess of 11/04, now with numbers. BFS reads some per-vertex data in random
   order:
   - `distance` once per edge;
   - `parent` and the start of the vertex's edges once per vertex. For CSR the start is in
     `offsets`; for the adjacency list it is in the 24-byte `std::vector` header.

   That makes 12 bytes per vertex for CSR and 32 bytes for the adjacency list. Compared with the
   16 MB L2 of the performance cores:

   | log2 n | CSR | Adjacency list | Ratio (G(n,m), R-MAT) |
   |-------:|----:|---------------:|----------------------:|
   | 18 | 3 MB | 8 MB | 1.23, 1.22 |
   | 19 | 6 MB | 16 MB | 1.85, 1.73 |
   | 20 | 12 MB | 32 MB | 1.87, 1.84 |
   | 21 | 24 MB | 64 MB | 1.32, 1.35 |

   The peak lies at exactly the sizes where CSR's data fits in L2 and the adjacency list's does
   not. With 2^18 vertices both fit; with 2^21, neither does. The grid shows no peak because its
   accesses are almost in order.

   This is still a correlation. A test: on a CPU with a different L2 size, the peak should move to
   the sizes where the same split happens. Cache-miss counters on the x86 laptop can show it
   directly.

   *Tested on the x86 laptop the same day: the peak came at 2^18 vertices, one size earlier than
   this count predicts for its 12 MB L3. See
   [`../x86-laptop/2026-11-11.md`](../x86-laptop/2026-11-11.md).*
3. On the grid with 2^19 vertices, the adjacency list is faster (0.96). This is the only such
   case in either run, and it has no explanation yet.
4. With 2^14 vertices and more, `std::queue` costs 7–33 % more than the flat array, as on
   11/04.
