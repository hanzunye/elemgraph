# Q4 and Q1 on the x86 laptop, 2026-11-11

This is the first run on the second machine, with hardware counters. It ran the same benchmark
code as the M2 run of the same day ([`../m2/2026-11-11.md`](../m2/2026-11-11.md)); only comments
changed in between. Raw results are in [`2026-11-11.json`](2026-11-11.json). The figures that
compare both machines are [`../q4.svg`](../q4.svg) and [`../q1.svg`](../q1.svg).

## Setup

- Intel Core i7-10750H (Comet Lake, 6 cores, 2 threads per core): L1d 32 KB and L2 256 KB per
  core, L3 12 MB shared. Linux on bare metal, other services stopped.
- `bench/run.sh x86-laptop`, run under `setarch -R` (address space randomization off). The
  settings it recorded:
  - pinned to CPU 2. SMT stayed on; the other thread of the same core, CPU 8, was not reserved.
  - governor `performance`, turbo off, so the clock stays at 2.6 GHz.
  - on AC power, load average 0.02 at the start.
- GCC 16.2.0 with libstdc++, `-O3 -DNDEBUG`, commit `09c5b4e`. The M2 used Clang 21 with libc++.
  So any difference between the two machines can come from the CPU or from the compiler.
- 10 repetitions; all tables show medians.
- Variation within the run: the coefficient of variation is at most 1.8 % with 2^14 vertices and
  more, and at most 3.3 % with 2^12. Variation between runs is not known yet, since this machine
  has run the suite once.
- Hardware counters: cycles, instructions, cache misses and branch mispredictions. They come from
  Google Benchmark with libpfm and count user mode only, per iteration of the timed loop. The
  tables divide them by n.
  - Instructions are the same in every repetition, to 0.05 %.
  - *Cache misses* is Linux's generic event. On this CPU it counts requests that miss the L3,
    including some made by the hardware prefetchers. It is good for comparisons, not as the
    number of loads that wait for memory.

### Reading

1. **Which algorithm is faster after the search depends on the machine.** Here the chain
   decomposition is faster in 10 of 24 cases; on the M2 it was 4 of 24.
   - On the grid it is 2–6 % faster at every size up to 2^21 and equal at 2^22. On the M2 it was
     1.2–1.3× slower there.
   - On G(n,m) it is 3–6 % faster with 2^18 to 2^20 vertices, and 1–8 % slower at the other
     sizes.
   - On R-MAT it is 1.12–1.35× slower. On the M2 that was 1.07–1.93×.
2. **The guess from the M2 is wrong.** It said that in cache, the chain decomposition loses
   because it executes more instructions. In fact, it executes 11–13 % *fewer* instructions than
   the lowpoint algorithm on every graph and at every size: about 107 instead of 123 per vertex on
   the grid, 171 instead of 195 on G(n,m), and 253–290 instead of 285–327 on R-MAT. This holds
   for GCC on this machine; the M2 has no counters.
3. **On R-MAT, the chain decomposition mispredicts many more branches.** It has 4.3–4.8 branch
   mispredictions per vertex, against 1.5–2.3 for the lowpoint algorithm. A misprediction costs
   about 15–20 cycles on this core. So the extra 2.5–2.7 per vertex account for about 35–55 of the
   60–80 extra cycles per vertex in cache (2^14 and 2^16). On G(n,m) the counts are close (3.2
   against 3.1), and on the grid both are near zero. The counters do not show which branches
   these are; a profile with `perf record` can.
4. **Where the chain decomposition wins on G(n,m), it also misses the L3 less.** At 2^18, 2^19 and
   2^20 it has 1.0, 5.6 and 16.1 cache misses per vertex, against 4.2, 10.5 and 23.0 for the
   lowpoint algorithm. At 2^21 and 2^22 the counts are close, and so are the times.
5. **With the search included, the two stay close.** The whole computation with the chain
   decomposition takes 0.97–1.17× as long as with the lowpoint algorithm, and 0.98–1.08× from
   2^20 vertices on. On the M2 that was 0.98–1.09×.
6. **The DFS executes 3.0–3.5× as many instructions per vertex as BFS** on the same graph (139 vs.
   47 on the grid, 219 vs. 71 on G(n,m), 336–367 vs. 99–105 on R-MAT). On G(n,m) and R-MAT it takes
   1.8–2.4× as long as BFS (2^14 vertices and more). On the grid from 2^19 vertices on, it is
   3.6–5× *faster* than BFS, for the reason in Q1, reading 4.

### Reading

1. **There is a peak here too, but one size earlier than on the M2.** It lies at 2^18 vertices for
   all three classes (grid 1.75, G(n,m) 1.54, R-MAT 1.33). On G(n,m) and R-MAT it is still
   1.28–1.41 at 2^19, then falls off. On the M2 the peak was at 2^19 and 2^20, and the grid had
   none.
2. **The difference is in memory, not in the work done.** Both representations execute the same
   instructions per vertex (to 0.1 %) with the same number of branch mispredictions. The time
   ratio equals the ratio of cycles. With 2^16 vertices, neither misses the L3. With 2^18 vertices,
   CSR misses it 0.02–1.3 times per vertex and the adjacency list 0.9–6.8 times. On G(n,m) and
   R-MAT, each extra miss costs only 15–28 cycles, much less than a memory access. So many misses
   are in flight at the same time.
3. **The prediction from the M2 failed.** The M2 notes counted the data that BFS reads in random
   order as 12 bytes per vertex for CSR and 32 for the adjacency list. They predicted that a CPU
   with other caches would show the peak where CSR's 12 bytes fit and the adjacency list's 32 do
   not. With the 12 MB L3, that means 2^19 vertices (6 MB and 16 MB), with 2^20 at the edge. The
   peak came at 2^18, where both fit (3 MB and 8 MB).

   The count left out the neighbor lists themselves. In G(n,m) and R-MAT, BFS also reaches them
   in an order unrelated to their place in memory. Counting all the data that BFS reads gives the
   following, in bytes per vertex and in MB at 2^18:

   | | CSR | Adjacency list |
   |-|----:|---------------:|
   | grid | 32 (8 MB) | 68 (17 MB) |
   | G(n,m) | 48 (12 MB) | about 82 (about 20.5 MB) |

   - CSR needs 4 bytes of `offsets`, 4 per neighbor in `targets`, and 12 for `distance`,
     `parent` and the queue.
   - The adjacency list needs the 24-byte vector header, the list in its own heap block, and the
     same 12 bytes. With glibc's malloc, a list of up to 6 neighbors takes a 32-byte block, and
     each 4 more neighbors take 16 bytes more; for degree 8 on average that is about 46 bytes.

   This count fits this machine: at 2^18, CSR fits in the 12 MB L3, just about for G(n,m), and
   the adjacency list does not. It does not fit the M2. With its 16 MB L2, the count puts the M2's
   peak at 2^18 as well, but there it lies at 2^19 and 2^20. So no single count explains both
   machines yet. Possible reasons on the M2 are its 8 MB system-level cache behind the L2, its
   16 KB pages and its prefetchers; without counters on the M2, none of them is tested.
4. **On the grid, BFS becomes 5× slower per vertex from 2^19 vertices on, on both
   representations.** On CSR it goes from 11.5 ns per vertex at 2^18 to 61 ns at 2^19 and 87 ns at
   2^22. On the M2 it only goes from 4.7 to 8.7 ns. With about one L3 miss per vertex, the cache
   misses cannot explain this. The DFS on the same grids takes 17 ns per vertex at every size from
   2^19 on.

   A guess: TLB misses.
   - The grid is stored row by row: 512 × 512 vertices at 2^18, 512 × 1024 at 2^19, 1024 × 1024 at
     2^20.
   - BFS starts near a corner and moves across the grid by diagonals. Two vertices that are next
     to each other on a diagonal lie one row minus one entry apart in each array.
   - From 2^19 on, a row of `distance` is 4 KB. So every vertex of the frontier lies on its own
     4 KB page in `distance`, `parent` and `offsets`, and in `targets` too.
   - A frontier of 512 vertices then touches about 2,000 pages, more than the 1,536 entries of the
     second-level TLB. At 2^18 it touches about 1,300.
   - In the adjacency list, the 24-byte headers and the 32-byte lists put every frontier vertex on
     its own page already at 2^18, so it would touch about 1,500 pages there. That could also be
     why its grid peak lies at 2^18.

   TLB-miss counters (`DTLB_LOAD_MISSES.WALK_COMPLETED`) or a run with transparent huge pages
   can check this.
5. On CSR with 2^14 vertices and more, BFS with `std::queue` takes 0.96–1.19× as long as with
   the flat array. On the M2 that was 1.07–1.33×.
